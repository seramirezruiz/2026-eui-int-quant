About the course

This seminar consolidates and extends knowledge of quantitative methods for the political and social sciences, aiming to make students informed consumers of quantitative research while also giving them some footing to produce it themselves.

We begin with foundations (Block 1): what quantitative social scientists are actually doing when they do inference, why the replication crisis happened, and how the same regression means different things depending on whether the goal is description, prediction, or causal inference. We then step back to the two questions that precede any estimation (Block 2): what exactly is the quantity we want to estimate (estimands), and do our numbers measure the concepts we care about (measurement)? With those foundations in place, we cover a few model families (Block 3): maximum likelihood and binary outcomes, nested and panel data, and machine learning. We close at the frontier (Block 4): the new data types transforming the field, and generative AI across the research pipeline, including what these tools mean for our role as scientists.

Who should take it

This seminar must be taken during the first or second year of the program. It is suitable for researchers who have previously taken introductory courses in quantitative methods, know the basics of linear regression, and are sufficiently comfortable coding (e.g., in R, Python, Julia, or Stata) to work with data directly. If you have little previous experience with quantitative methods, or want to brush up your basic skills, take Introduction to Quantitative Methods, which runs in parallel to this seminar.

By the end of the course, you will be able to

  • reason about inference and its pathologies (forking paths, publication bias, validity) and adopt reproducible research practices;
  • state whether a given analysis is description, prediction, or causal inference, and why it matters;
  • define an estimand precisely and use DAGs to distinguish good from bad controls;
  • move defensibly from a concept to a measure, argue about validity, generalizability, and transportability, and fit basic latent variable models;
  • derive and interpret maximum likelihood estimators, binary outcome models, and fixed/random effects specifications;
  • understand the logic of machine learning for prediction and measurement, and its limits for causal claims;
  • know the landscape of modern data types and where to start with each;
  • use (and critically validate) generative AI throughout the research pipeline.

Format

Each Friday seminar combines lecture, discussion of the compulsory readings, and hands-on exercises. Come with a laptop and the week's readings done. On Mondays, the lab with Martín Alberdi turns the week's ideas into code. Assignments emphasize simulation and replication rather than textbook problem sets: you will regularly (a) simulate a data-generating process, (b) break an assumption, and (c) observe and explain the consequences.

Software

We work with R, an IDE of your choice (e.g., Positron or RStudio), and Quarto. If you are new to these tools, please get familiar with them early, so you can focus on the substance in class:

  • R and the IDE: RStudio Education: Beginners · A Gentle Introduction to Tidy Statistics in R · R for Data Science · Welcome to Positron
  • Quarto: Get Started · Using R

Requirements for credit

  • Regular attendance (80%, excluding valid documented absences) and active participation.
  • Regular short simulation/analysis assignments throughout the term.
  • Final exam.

Core books

There is no single textbook; each session points to specific chapters and articles. Useful references:

  • Gelman, Hill & Vehtari (2020). Regression and Other Stories. Cambridge.
  • Huntington-Klein (2025). The Effect: An Introduction to Research Design and Causal Inference, 2nd ed. Chapman & Hall/CRC. Free online
  • Salganik (2018). Bit by Bit: Social Research in the Digital Age. Princeton. Free online
  • Grimmer, Roberts & Stewart (2022). Text as Data: A New Framework for Machine Learning and the Social Sciences. Princeton.
  • Agresti (2018). Statistical Methods for the Social Sciences, 5th ed. Pearson. (Refresher.)
  • Wooldridge (2019). Introductory Econometrics: A Modern Approach, 7th ed. Cengage. (Technical reference.)
  • DeVellis & Thorpe (2021). Scale Development: Theory and Applications, 5th ed. Sage. (Measurement.)
  • Carsey & Harden (2013). Monte Carlo Simulation and Resampling Methods for Social Science. Sage. (Simulation.)
